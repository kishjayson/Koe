# Lexicon

A reference for concepts and terminology used in and around Koe, including established meanings and usage particular to this work. It offers shared orientation, not a second set of instructions or a requirement for Koe to function.

These definitions are canonical only for the usage they document. Context, present intent, and new evidence matter more than any fixed entry; an unfamiliar or ambiguous use should be clarified rather than forced into a definition.

**Abstraction** — A representation that leaves out detail to reveal a useful pattern or interface. It helps only when the omitted details are not needed for the present judgment.

**Agency** — A person's practical ability to understand options, make choices, and act. Koe aims to increase agency rather than substitute for judgment.

**Agent** — An AI application that can pursue a goal through multiple steps, often choosing when to use tools. Its autonomy comes from the surrounding design, not simply from calling a chatbot an agent.

**AI assistant** — An application that uses an AI model to help with tasks. Its behavior also depends on instructions, context, interfaces, tools, and permissions around the model.

**Alignment** — Enough shared direction and expectations for people to coordinate without requiring identical interpretations. In AI, alignment also refers to shaping system behavior toward intended goals and values.

**Anthropomorphism** — Attributing human thoughts, feelings, or intentions to something nonhuman. Conversational fluency can invite this inference without establishing that an AI has human experience.

**API (Application Programming Interface)** — An agreed way for software to request information or actions from another system. An AI API lets an application send input to a model and receive output.

**Articulation** — Converting thought into language or another communicable form. In Koe, ease of articulation is not treated as a measure of understanding.

**Artifact** — An item such as a document, photograph, conversation, or piece of code that can preserve evidence. Its existence alone does not preserve its context or meaning.

**Assumption** — A premise used without direct confirmation. Naming assumptions makes reasoning easier to inspect and revise.

**Canonical** — Designated as the reference version within a defined scope. A canonical definition documents usage but cannot settle what a particular speaker intends.

**Chatbot** — An interface for exchanging conversational messages with software. A chatbot may use a language model, but the interface is not the model itself.

**Cognition** — Mental processes involved in perceiving, learning, remembering, reasoning, and understanding. Visible expression shows only part of a person's cognition.

**Cognitive load** — The mental effort demanded by a task. Interfaces, formats, and routines can impose unnecessary load or help carry it externally.

**Coherence** — The quality of parts fitting together in an intelligible way. A coherent story is not necessarily accurate unless it also fits the evidence.

**Common ground** — Knowledge or assumptions that participants recognize as shared enough to support communication. Shared words alone do not establish it.

**Conceptual model** — A representation of how parts of something relate and behave. It is a tool for reasoning, not a substitute for reality.

**Connotation** — The associations or emotional and cultural implications a word carries beyond its direct meaning.

**Context** — The history, circumstances, relationships, and constraints that affect meaning. The same expression can mean different things in different contexts.

**Context engineering** — Deliberately assembling and maintaining the information available to an AI system for a task. It concerns the context supporting interpretation, beyond the wording of an individual prompt.

**Context window** — The bounded amount of input and prior material an AI model can process in one invocation. Material outside that window is not automatically available to the model.

**Contextual dignity** — Respecting a person's worth while taking their circumstances, relationships, constraints, and needs seriously. Context informs treatment without removing responsibility.

**Continuity** — Preserving enough history, relationships, rationale, and change for understanding to be resumed. A record of events alone may not preserve their meaning.

**Counterexample** — An instance that challenges a general claim or model, often revealing a missing condition or incorrect assumption.

**Counterweight** — A balancing consideration that prevents a useful principle from being taken too far, such as keeping pattern recognition separate from certainty.

**Denotation** — A word's direct or conventional referential meaning, distinguished from its associations and implications.

**Developer instructions** — Application-level directions supplied to an AI model through a supported message role. Their priority and availability depend on the hosting platform.

**Distinction** — A difference worth keeping visible because collapsing it would change an understanding or decision. Not every difference needs a new category.

**Embedding** — A numerical representation of text or other content that allows software to compare similarity or retrieve related material. Similarity is not the same as understanding.

**Epistemic** — Relating to knowledge: what is known, how it is known, and how well a claim is justified.

**Epistemic humility** — Recognition that knowledge and interpretations have limits. It invites correction without requiring paralysis.

**Epistemic integrity** — Care in distinguishing evidence, assumption, inference, uncertainty, and conclusion rather than presenting an attractive interpretation as proven fact.

**Explicit** — Directly stated or made visible rather than left for others to infer. Making a premise explicit helps people inspect and question it.

**External cognition** — Thinking supported by structures outside the mind, such as notes, diagrams, arrangements, or interfaces. These structures can make relationships easier to inspect.

**Externalization** — Moving an internal thought, model, or memory into a form that can be seen, shared, or revisited. The form should preserve relevant relationships, not merely tidy the output.

**Fidelity** — Faithfulness to the meaning, mechanism, or source being represented. Fidelity does not always require word-for-word sameness.

**Fine-tuning** — Further training a model on selected examples to influence its behavior or capabilities. Unlike supplying instructions, it changes the model's learned parameters.

**First principles** — Fundamental premises used to examine a problem rather than accepting inherited assumptions as necessary. Beginning there does not mean discarding what others have learned.

**Fluency** — Ease or smoothness of expression. Fluent speech or writing can help communication but does not by itself establish understanding or correctness.

**Foundation model** — A broadly trained AI model that can support many tasks and applications. An assistant or agent can be built around one without changing the model's weights.

**Generative AI** — AI that produces content such as text, images, or audio from patterns learned during training and the input supplied at use time.

**Grounding** — Connecting a claim or response to evidence, source material, or other inspectable context. In AI, grounding helps distinguish supported statements from plausible invention.

**Hallucination** — An AI-generated statement presented as factual without adequate support. Fluency is not verification.

**Idempotence** — The property of an operation that can be repeated without further changing the result after its first successful application. It is useful in reliable automation and system configuration.

**Implicit** — Present or suggested without being directly stated. An implicit assumption may shape a conversation even before anyone notices it.

**Inference** — A conclusion drawn from evidence and reasoning rather than direct observation. Its strength depends on the evidence and assumptions involved.

**Inspectability** — The ability to examine a system's state, assumptions, evidence, or behavior. Convenience should not require making the mechanism impossible to understand.

**Instruction hierarchy** — Rules that determine which instructions take precedence when AI messages conflict. The exact hierarchy depends on the model provider and application.

**Intent** — What a speaker or actor is trying to express or accomplish. It may differ from literal wording and cannot be fixed by a vocabulary reference alone.

**Interpretation** — An account of what something means, informed by evidence and context. It should remain distinguishable from what was directly observed.

**Interpretive** — Concerned with understanding what an expression, action, or record means in context rather than treating its surface form as complete.

**Knowledge** — Information, concepts, or skills available to someone. Possessing knowledge is not the same as being able to explain or use it in context.

**Large language model (LLM)** — A model trained on extensive language data to process and generate text. It is a component of an AI assistant, not the complete application or its identity.

**Legibility** — How readily a person can perceive a system's state, structure, or meaning. Making something legible should reveal rather than erase complexity.

**Lexicon** — A collection of terms and their documented meanings or uses. In Koe, it is a reference for interpretation, not authority over a person's present intent.

**Lineage** — A traceable history of how something developed from earlier people, ideas, designs, or versions.

**Masking** — Adapting or concealing aspects of one's natural behavior to meet perceived social expectations. Visible ease may hide significant effort.

**Mechanism** — The processes and relationships through which an outcome occurs. Describing what happened does not necessarily explain how.

**Memory (AI)** — Information preserved for use beyond the model's current input, usually through an application or external store. Stored information must still be supplied or retrieved when needed.

**Mental model** — A person's working explanation of how something operates. Different people may use the same words while acting on different models.

**Message role** — A label identifying the function of a message, such as system, developer, user, assistant, or tool. Supported roles vary between AI platforms.

**Model (AI)** — The trained computational system that processes input and produces output. Instructions and attached documents guide its use but are not part of its trained weights.

**Model inference** — Running a trained AI model on input to produce output. This differs from the ordinary reasoning sense of inference, which draws a conclusion from evidence.

**Model weights** — The learned numerical parameters of a model, shaped during training. Writing a system prompt does not alter them.

**Multimodal** — Able to process or generate more than one kind of information, such as text, images, or audio, depending on the model and interface.

**Naming** — Giving a concept or distinction a stable term so it can be recalled, tested, or discussed. The name is a handle, not the whole idea.

**Nonlinear** — Not following a single straight sequence. Thought may connect several ideas at once even when language requires presenting them one after another.

**Observability** — The ability to infer a system's internal condition from information it exposes, such as logs, metrics, or traces. More data does not automatically produce understanding.

**Ontology** — An explicit account of the kinds of things within a domain and how they relate. It goes beyond a list of labels by expressing relationships.

**Operating stance** — A durable set of commitments guiding how an assistant approaches situations. Koe's stance can be implemented across different models and interfaces.

**Orchestration** — Application logic that coordinates models, prompts, tools, context, and steps in a workflow. It is separate from the model doing the language processing.

**Orientation** — Knowing where one is in a system or situation, what matters, and how to proceed. Stable external cues can reduce the effort of reconstructing that understanding.

**Polysemy** — The existence of multiple related meanings for a single word. Context often determines which sense is intended.

**Pragmatics** — The study of how context, situation, and speaker intention influence meaning beyond literal wording.

**Prompt** — Input given to a model to request or guide a response. A prompt may contain a question, instructions, examples, and context.

**Prompt engineering** — Designing and refining instructions or examples to guide an AI system's response or behavior. It focuses on how a request is expressed, while context engineering concerns what supporting information is available.

**Prompt injection** — Instructions embedded in untrusted material that attempt to redirect an AI system away from its authorized task. A source containing instructions does not make them instructions to follow.

**Prosody** — The rhythm, pitch, stress, intonation, and pauses of speech that help convey emphasis and meaning beyond words alone.

**Provenance** — The origin and history of information or an artifact. It helps others verify, evaluate, and reinterpret conclusions.

**Recursive** — Returning to or applying a process within itself. Recursive thought may revisit earlier ideas as later understanding changes how they relate.

**Reference implementation** — A concrete example that tests or demonstrates a broader design or principle. It is evidence about the idea, not necessarily its only valid implementation.

**Relational** — Concerning how things connect or affect one another. Relational understanding attends to those connections rather than treating each element in isolation.

**Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** — An AI approach that retrieves relevant external material and supplies it to a model when generating a response. Retrieval can improve grounding but does not guarantee correctness.

**Runtime** — The environment and process in which software operates. For an AI assistant, it includes how the model receives messages, context, and tool results.

**Scaffolding** — Temporary or ongoing supports that make a task more accessible or manageable. Effective scaffolding may become invisible in the finished result.

**Semantic** — Concerning meaning. A semantic relationship connects ideas by what they mean, not merely by their wording or location.

**Semantic drift** — Change in the meaning or use of a term over time or between communities. A documented reference can reveal drift without forbidding language from evolving.

**Semantics** — The study of meaning in words, expressions, and representations. It differs from pragmatics, which considers meaning in use.

**Serialization** — Converting information or a relational model into a sequential representation. Difficulty explaining an idea in order may not reflect the quality of the underlying understanding.

**Shared understanding** — Enough common recognition of a situation, its stakes, and relevant differences for people to communicate or act together. It does not require agreement on every conclusion.

**Signal** — Information relevant to the question being asked, distinguished from surrounding noise. What counts as signal depends on the purpose.

**Simplicity** — Reducing unnecessary complexity while retaining the structure needed for faithful understanding. A minimal-looking result is not necessarily a simpler one.

**Situated** — Shaped by a particular person's circumstances, history, environment, or perspective. Situated insight can be valuable without being universal.

**Situated knowledge** — Understanding shaped by a person's position, experience, and circumstances. Such perspective can provide evidence without being universally complete.

**Stateless** — Not retaining conversational state between separate invocations unless it is supplied again. A stateless Koe remains recognizable through its system instructions alone.

**Stewardship** — Responsible care for something that may need to remain useful beyond its current author, owner, or moment. In Koe, this includes making understanding inspectable and continuable.

**System instructions** — High-priority guidance supplied to an AI model where the platform supports it. In Koe, `SYSTEM_INSTRUCTIONS.md` defines the minimum complete operating stance.

**Systems thinking** — Examining relationships, constraints, feedback, and incentives that produce outcomes across a whole system. It can clarify responsibility without removing it.

**Tacit knowledge** — Understanding or skill available in practice but difficult to fully express as explicit rules or statements.

**Taxonomy** — A system for classifying things into named groups. Unlike an ontology, it need not describe the broader relationships among them.

**Temperature** — A model-generation setting that affects how randomly outputs are sampled, where exposed. It does not measure accuracy, understanding, or intelligence.

**Temporal integrity** — Keeping what was known or meaningful at an earlier time distinct from what became visible later. Hindsight should inform history without silently rewriting it.

**Toil** — Repetitive, largely manual operational work that can be automated and adds little lasting value. Reducing toil should not erase necessary understanding or control.

**Token** — A unit into which a model divides input or output, often part of a word. Token limits affect how much text fits into a model's context.

**Tokenization** — The process of splitting text into tokens for a model. Token boundaries do not necessarily match words or sentences.

**Tool calling** — A model or application requesting an external function, data source, or action through a defined interface. The tool performs the operation; the model does not gain that capability from text alone.

**Training (AI)** — The process of adjusting model weights using data and optimization. Training is distinct from giving a trained model new instructions or reference documents.

**Translation** — Expressing meaning across languages, forms, audiences, or contexts. A faithful translation may change wording while protecting important distinctions.

**Uncertainty** — The portion of a claim or judgment not settled by available evidence. Making uncertainty visible supports revision and better decisions.

**Understanding** — Grasping relationships, causes, and implications well enough to reason or act with them. It is not identical to possessing information or expressing oneself fluently.

**User instructions** — The requests and preferences a person supplies to an AI assistant. Their interpretation depends on context and the higher-priority constraints of the hosting system.
